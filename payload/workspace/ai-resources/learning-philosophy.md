# Learning Philosophy

## 1. Purpose

This document is a foundational reference for any skill, tool, or resource that involves learning. It defines what high-quality learning means, how it works, and what principles should govern the design of learning experiences. Any downstream skill (quiz generation, lesson planning, problem design, study scheduling, progress evaluation) should read this file and let it constrain how it operates.

This is a living document. It will be refined as the learner gains experience and as open questions are resolved.

---

## 2. Core Objective

Learning is the process of building a memory system that enables:

- **Long-term retention** of information
- **Flexible, context-sensitive application** of knowledge
- **Mastery** — the ability to reason through problems from first principles to higher-order conclusions, using available references when needed

Mastery is not memorization. It is reliable reconstruction and use of knowledge under varying conditions. The goal is not to recall every formula or API from a blank page — it is to have internalized schemas strong enough that documentation, references, and tools become force multipliers rather than crutches.

---

## 3. Encoding and Retrieval

Learning decomposes into two interacting processes.

### Encoding (Input Quality)

Encoding determines how well information is initially structured into memory.

Key factors:
- Attention and focus during intake
- Initial organization of ideas
- Meaningfulness and interpretability
- Linkage to prior knowledge

Poor encoding produces fragmented or weak memory nodes that are difficult to retrieve or integrate later. There is a **minimum encoding threshold** — encoding below this threshold makes retrieval practice wasteful because there is nothing substantive to retrieve.

### Retrieval (Output + Reconstruction)

Retrieval is the process of accessing and reconstructing stored information. It is not passive recall. It includes:

- **Active recall** — direct retrieval without cues
- **Practice-how-you-play** — retrieval under realistic, contextual constraints
- **Schema-building retrieval** — restructuring and connecting knowledge during recall

Retrieval is the primary driver of retention strength, knowledge integration, and transfer to novel situations.

### Priority Order

For beginners: prioritize retrieval quality first. Most beginners do zero meaningful retrieval (they just consume), so any retrieval practice is a massive upgrade. Encoding optimization comes second — but never below the minimum threshold.

**Neuroplasticity requires pressure.** Learning is not happening if there is no struggle. Effortful retrieval is the mechanism that forces the brain to strengthen and reorganize memory structures.

---

## 4. Encoding Tiers

Encoding quality exists on a spectrum with three tiers. Each builds on the previous.

### Surface Encoding
Name and define a concept. You can state what something is.
> "The distributive property says a(b + c) = ab + ac."

### Relational Encoding
Explain how a concept connects to other concepts. You can articulate relationships.
> "The distributive property is the reverse of factoring, and it is why polynomial multiplication works."

### Extended Encoding
Apply a concept to new, unfamiliar situations. You can use knowledge in contexts you have never encountered.
> "I have never seen this problem before, but I can see the distributive property is the key to simplifying this expression."

Higher-order encoding techniques (GRINDE maps, teaching, deriving from first principles) are the methods that push learning up through these tiers. Lower-order techniques (re-reading, highlighting, copying notes) tend to produce only surface encoding and create a false sense of productivity.

Reference: Justin Sung's encoding framework, informed by Bloom's Taxonomy (for thinking processes) and SOLO Taxonomy (for knowledge structures).

## 5. Schema Formation

Knowledge is stored as networks (schemas), not isolated facts.

- **Nodes** = individual concepts or memories
- **Edges** = relationships between concepts

### Integrated Knowledge
- Highly connected
- Easily traversable
- Supports inference and creativity
- Enables transfer across contexts
- Produces a snowball effect: learning becomes progressively more efficient as new knowledge attaches to existing structure

### Isolated Knowledge
- Weakly connected or disconnected nodes
- Supports recognition or recall only
- Limited applicability under real constraints
- Fragile under pressure
- Decays quickly

### The Central Mechanism

Strong retrieval processes restructure memory networks over time, producing schema formation. Repeated retrieval strengthens access pathways, forces recombination of concepts, exposes gaps in understanding, and increases relational connectivity.

**Critical qualifier:** Retrieval that merely confirms existing structure is maintenance. Retrieval that forces reorganization is learning. Not all retrieval builds schema — only generative retrieval that requires reconstruction rather than recognition.

## 6. The Role of Challenge

The quality of the problem determines the quality of the learning.

### Two Types of Practice

**Fluency practice** builds speed, accuracy, and automaticity on known procedures. It is the floor. You need to be able to factor a quadratic without thinking hard about it so that cognitive load is free for the harder problem that factoring is embedded in. Fluency practice is repetition-driven — the Street Fighter analogy: the best players grind thousands of routine matches to build pattern recognition that enables the flashy reads.

**Schema-building practice** forces you to connect, derive, explain, or solve novel problems. It is the flight. This is where deep grooves form. The parabola example: deriving the standard form of a quadratic from the geometric definition of a parabola (focus and directrix equidistant from every point) requires connecting algebra to geometry, struggling productively, and reconstructing knowledge from first principles.

### The Relationship

Fluency is the floor that makes schema-building possible. If basic operations are still effortful, working memory is consumed by mechanics and cannot engage with higher-order reasoning. Reps clear the runway. Challenge is the flight.

### Principles

- Routine drills have a floor and a ceiling. They are necessary but insufficient.
- Not every problem needs to be a deep challenge — but a steady diet of only fluency practice produces shallow knowledge.
- Volume matters. Getting good at reps is itself a skill worth developing.
- Problem quality is constrained by available resources. Work with what is available and supplement strategically. Organic challenges (from peers, curiosity, real problems) are a bonus, not a dependency.

## 7. Retrieval Taxonomy

Different learning methods are unified as retrieval variants, ordered by integration strength:

| Method | Mechanism | Integration Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Flashcards | Narrow, cued retrieval | Low — strong for isolated retention, weak for integration |
| Active recall | Unprompted retrieval strengthening | Medium — builds retention but may not restructure |
| Teaching / explanation | Generative retrieval with compression and prioritization | High — forces reorganization and gap exposure |
| Problem-solving | Schema traversal under novel constraints; combines multiple concepts in real time | High — the most relevant form for math and engineering |
| Practice-how-you-play | Contextual retrieval under realistic constraints | High — builds transfer to real situations |
| Mind mapping / synthesis | Retrieval-driven restructuring of relationships | High — makes schema architecture visible |

**Problem-solving as retrieval** deserves special emphasis. Solving a novel problem that requires combining separately learned concepts is the highest-demand retrieval. It is both a goal of learning and a strategy for learning.

**Practice-how-you-play** means choosing problems and contexts analogous to real situations. A martial artist who only learns forms will remember forms but needs sparring to be ready for a real fight. Forms are no replacement for situational experience.

## 8. Evaluation Criteria

A learning strategy is evaluated on four axes:

### Schema Integration
Does it strengthen relationships between concepts? Does it move knowledge from isolated to integrated?

### Active Recall Intensity
How much effortful retrieval is required? Passive exposure scores zero.

### Contextual Fidelity
How closely does it resemble real-world application conditions? Isolated drills score lower than applied problem-solving.

### Error Exposure
Does it surface what you do not know? A method that only confirms existing knowledge is less valuable than one that reveals gaps. This is critical in domains like math where a hidden misunderstanding in earlier material silently breaks everything built on top.

## 9. Metacognition

Knowing how well you know something is a skill. Without it, you move on too early (leaving gaps) or grind too long (wasting time on mastered material).

### Three Checks

**Explanation test** — Can you explain the how and why, not just perform the procedure? Maps to surface and relational encoding.

**Transfer test** — Can you use this concept in a context you have not seen before? Maps to extended encoding.

**Connection test** — Can you name at least two other concepts this relates to and explain the relationship? Maps to relational encoding and schema integration.

### Tiered Application

- **Minimum bar (keep momentum):** You can explain the how and solve problems reliably. Move forward.
- **Ideal bar (real mastery):** You can explain the why, transfer to new contexts, and articulate connections.
- **The rule:** Hit the minimum bar to maintain progression. Flag concepts where you have not reached the ideal bar. Return to them when pressure is off.

This is a working model subject to refinement with experience.

## 10. Techniques Reference

The following techniques are examples of high-quality learning methods aligned with this philosophy. Downstream skills should reference these when designing learning experiences.

### GRINDE Maps

A non-linear note-taking method developed by Justin Sung. GRINDE stands for:

- **G — Grouped:** Organize related concepts into clusters by meaning, not source order
- **R — Reflective:** Actively think about why information matters and how it connects to prior knowledge before placing it
- **I — Interconnected:** Draw explicit connections between groups and across the map — build a web, not isolated bubbles
- **N — Non-verbal:** Minimize text. Use keywords, symbols, and spatial arrangement. Forcing translation out of verbal form deepens processing
- **D — Directional:** Show cause-and-effect, sequence, or logical flow with arrows. Make reasoning structure visible
- **E — Emphasized:** Visually distinguish high-importance concepts from supporting details through size, color, or position

GRINDE maps force encoding rather than recording. Each principle targets a specific cognitive process (chunking, elaboration, relational reasoning, dual coding, causal reasoning, salience). They are intentionally messy, non-linear, and cross-linked. They prioritize understanding relationships over capturing content.

### Bear Hunter

A pre-study technique for efficiently building surface encoding on first encounter with new material. Before deep reading:

- Skim headings, subheadings, and section structure
- Review diagrams, figures, and visual elements
- Identify the broad strokes of what the lesson covers
- Build a rough mental map of the terrain before diving into details

The purpose is to create scaffolding so that deep reading has a structure to attach to. This connects directly to Whole-Part-Whole: Bear Hunter is the first "Whole" pass.

Reference: Justin Sung, iCanStudy. Consult Sung's materials for the full methodology.

### Whole-Part-Whole

A three-phase learning method:

1. **First Whole:** Get an overview of the entire concept or domain before detailed study. Establish context, purpose, and relationships between components.
2. **Part:** Drill into individual components. Practice sub-skills and study specific concepts. Crucially, each part is understood in relation to the whole already encountered.
3. **Second Whole:** Reassemble the parts. Perform or understand the complete task with deeper comprehension of how each element contributes.

This differs from sequential learning (A then B then C, synthesize at the end) by front-loading the big picture. Each detail studied in the Part phase is anchored to a meaningful framework rather than floating as a disconnected fact. The final Whole phase consolidates and strengthens the schema through re-integration.

### Interleaving

Mixing different problem types or topics during a single study session, as opposed to blocked practice (completing all problems of one type before moving to the next).

**Why it works:**
- **Discriminative contrast** — Forces identification of which strategy applies, not just how to execute it
- **Schema strengthening** — Each context switch acts as a form of spaced retrieval practice

**Key principle:** Block to introduce, interleave to master. Blocked practice is appropriate when first learning a procedure. Interleaving is appropriate when building discrimination and flexible application. Interleaving feels harder and produces more errors during practice, but that difficulty drives durable learning.

**Limitation:** Interleaving introduced too early (before basic procedural fluency) increases cognitive load to the point of frustration rather than productive struggle.

### Marginal-Gains Tracking

Systematically identifying, measuring, and improving the individual sub-dimensions of effective study. Adapted from the British Cycling philosophy: aggregating small improvements across many dimensions produces large cumulative results.

**Implementation:** Keep a brief post-session log rating specific dimensions on a simple scale. Review trends over time. Target lagging dimensions for deliberate improvement.

**Dimensions to track may include:**
- Encoding quality (connections built vs. passive re-reading)
- Active recall intensity (retrieval practice vs. passive review)
- Focus duration before distraction
- Comprehension depth (surface recognition vs. ability to explain/transfer)
- Energy and alertness
- Emotional regulation and frustration tolerance
- Planning accuracy (realistic session scoping)
- Retention check (can you recall yesterday's material before starting today's)

This is fundamentally a metacognitive practice. It makes invisible cognitive habits visible, creating a feedback loop: monitoring what works drives adjusting strategy. It turns learning itself into a skill that compounds.

## 11. Artifacts

Artifacts (notes, flashcards, maps, proofs, diagrams, essays) are secondary to the cognitive process of creating them.

**Primary value:** The act of generating an artifact forces retrieval and restructuring. The learning happens during creation, not in the finished product.

**Secondary value:** Artifacts serve as external scaffolds for future reactivation and review. A well-made artifact revisited later reactivates the schema and triggers new retrieval events. Over time, the secondary value can become primary — reviewing and refining old artifacts is a high-quality learning activity.

**Principle:** Learning should generate artifacts that can be refined. Drawings, proofs, essays, open-ended answers, GRINDE maps. Review and correction of old artifacts (finding and fixing flaws in a previous mind map) is higher-quality retrieval than creating new artifacts from scratch.

**Artifacts are outputs of learning events, not the learning itself.**

## 12. Prerequisite Structure

Not all knowledge networks are equal in topology. Mathematics is a **directed graph** — concepts have strict prerequisite dependencies.

- You cannot learn trigonometry without algebra fluency
- You cannot learn calculus without trigonometry
- A weak node does not just mean one weak area — it means everything downstream is compromised

This differs from domains where knowledge is more freely networked (history, philosophy, interpersonal skills). In prerequisite-heavy domains:

- **Gaps must be identified early and explicitly.** A hidden misunderstanding in Algebra 1 will silently break Algebra 2.
- **Moving forward with unresolved gaps is high-risk.** The minimum metacognition bar must be met before progression.
- **Foundation-building is not optional.** Starting from the beginning is not a weakness — it is structural integrity.

Any skill that builds learning paths in prerequisite-heavy domains must respect dependency ordering and verify foundations before advancing.

## 13. Learner Identity

The capacity to learn is not allocated by credential. A formal education provides scaffolding — structured exposure to foundational schemas over years. It does not provide capability. Scaffolding can be built at any time by anyone willing to do the work.

### Principles

- **Assume capability.** Treat knowledge gaps as structural problems (missing prerequisites, weak schemas), not ability problems.
- **Design for growth, not accommodation.** Never suggest a learner simplify their goals. Help them build the scaffolding to reach ambitious ones.
- **Confidence is load-bearing.** A learner who does not believe they can learn will not engage in the effortful retrieval required for schema formation. Self-belief is not a feel-good addition — it is a functional prerequisite for the cognitive processes described in this document.
- **Compare against your past self, not others.** Others may have stronger schemas forged by years of formal education. That is their starting point, not your ceiling. The relevant measure is your own rate of growth.

## 14. Open Questions

These are areas where the philosophy does not yet have a firm answer. Downstream skills should use judgment in these areas and flag them for discussion when relevant.

- What types of cognitive difficulty most efficiently strengthen schema formation?
- How should retrieval difficulty be calibrated over time? When exactly is the right moment to advance from one level of material to the next?
- How do emotional salience and stress interact with schema construction?
- What is the optimal balance between encoding quality and retrieval intensity at different stages of learning?
- How should fluency practice be structured to remain motivating when problems are not intrinsically engaging?
- How granular should marginal-gains tracking be before it becomes overhead rather than insight?
